Keep the four links ahead of your shelves i

Each ring shows how much a link can handle compared with what your shelves can sell. Green means it keeps up, red means it is holding you back. On Shelf space, the ring shows how full your shelves are. You sell whatever is smallest: what the shelves hold, what customers want (about 3 units each), what checkout can ring up, and what the crew can restock. Each aisle is its own ladder: own 10 of an item to unlock the next one. An item holds at most 25 copies, so you have to keep moving up. Store prices rise as you climb the parts list, so a store buy costs about what a part does.

How the shop works i

The shop finishes whatever is smallest: the work you have booked, the trucks coming in (about 4 labor hours each), what the writers can schedule, what the technicians can turn, and what the tools allow. Booked jobs take bay slots. Each section is its own ladder: own 10 of an item to unlock the next one, and each item holds at most 25 copies. Each ring shows how much a part of the shop can handle compared with the work your bays are booked for. Red means it is holding you back.

How it all fits together

The game is a chain. Money only flows as fast as the weakest link, so the puzzle is always: find the weak link, fix it, and see where the new one is.

Shelves→Customers→Checkout→Money
1. Selling parts

Every product you stock sells some units each second. You only sell as much as the smallest of four things: what's on your shelves, what customers want, what checkout can ring up, and what stocking can put back out. The red box at the top of the screen is the one holding you back right now.

2. Climbing the parts list

Each part starts with the cheapest brand and climbs to the best, one truck or engine at a time. Stock 10 of a product to unlock the next. Stock 25 of a part's last product to move on to the next part. A product holds at most 25, so piling up cheap old stock only goes so far. Every step costs more than the one before it.

3. Keeping up in the Store

More products means more units to move, so checkout, stocking and customers fall behind. Every product also takes shelf space. The Store tab is where you fix that. Each aisle is its own ladder: own 10 of an item to unlock the next. An item holds at most 25, so you can't just pile up cheap old gear. You have to move up.

4. The service shop

A few hours in, you can open a shop out back. Jobs earn money like parts do, but they cost more and pay back slower. The shop has its own chain: booked work, trucks coming in, service writers, technicians, and tools. The smallest one sets the pace. Booked jobs take bay slots, the way facings take shelf space. You build all of that in the Shop tab.

5. Where the two sides meet

Once the shop is open, every new part also needs the shop to keep up. You can't sell parts nobody installs. So you can't just race up the parts list: when a part is waiting on the shop, push the shop up, and it pays you back while it grows. The shop also earns close to half your income once it's running.

6. Things that go wrong

Spills and register jams slow checkout. Shoplifters take money. Backorders stop a department restocking for a while. In the shop, a broken lift cuts your tools, a sick technician cuts your labor, and a comeback costs money. Tap the banner (or the spill or thief in the picture) to fix what can be fixed. The rest clear up on their own.

7. A good habit

Glance at the red box, fix that link, then spend the rest on the next step of the parts list or the shop, whichever one the next part is waiting on. If a buy button is gray, you can't afford it yet, or there's no shelf or bay space left for it.
